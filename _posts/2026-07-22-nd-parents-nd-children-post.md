# Are Neurodivergent Parents More Likely to Have Neurodivergent Children?

## Genetic inheritance and family traits

Here's a question I get asked a lot, usually by someone squinting at their own family tree like it owes them an explanation: **if one parent is neurodivergent, are the kids more likely to be too?**

Short answer: yes, probably. Long answer: it's messier, more interesting, and more human than a simple yes.

### The short version of the science

Autism and ADHD are among the most heritable conditions in psychiatry. Twin and family studies consistently put autism's heritability somewhere in the 60–90% range, and ADHD isn't far behind. That doesn't mean there's a single "autism gene" waiting to be found and printed on a t-shirt. It means hundreds, possibly thousands, of small genetic variations combine to shift the odds — some inherited, some new in that particular child.

Family studies back this up in plain, unglamorous terms: if you're the parent of one autistic child, your chance of a second autistic child is meaningfully higher than the general population's. If a parent is autistic themselves, the odds shift again. Genetics loads the dice. It doesn't decide the roll on its own.

### Why so many of us find "the pattern" in hindsight

This is the part that isn't in the journals, but it's all over the group chats and comment sections: **the retrospective diagnosis spiral.** Parent gets a child assessed. Somewhere in that process, a switch flips, and suddenly they're looking at their own childhood — the meltdowns nobody named, the special interest that ran the whole house, the reason family gatherings always felt like performing a play they never got the script for.

I've watched this happen more times than I can count in this space. It's not that neurodivergence is contagious or newly fashionable. It's that an entire generation of adults grew up before assessment criteria caught up to what girls, women, and quieter presentations actually look like. Their kids are getting recognised. They're recognising themselves in the process.

### It runs in families, but it doesn't run in straight lines

Here's where it gets genuinely interesting rather than just statistically true. Neurodivergent traits don't copy-paste from parent to child. They *recombine*. You might have:

- An autistic parent and an ADHD child
- A parent with "just" some sensory quirks and a child who is profoundly autistic
- Two neurotypical parents and an autistic child, because genetics is a shuffled deck, not a photocopier
- Siblings who are both autistic but present so differently that strangers assume they're unrelated

Families are often a mixed bag of traits rather than a single diagnosis repeated down the line — a bit of anxiety here, a sensory sensitivity there, one relative who was "just eccentric," another who masks so well nobody's ever asked the question. Genetic inheritance in neurodivergence looks less like a family crest and more like a shared box of parts, assembled differently in every kid.

### What this actually means if you're the NT parent in the house

If you're the neurotypical partner watching this unfold — which is the seat I write from — this research isn't just academic trivia. It reframes a few things:

**It's not about blame.** "Where did this come from?" is a question that gets asked, sometimes gently, sometimes not, in every family navigating a new diagnosis. The honest answer is: from a combination of factors, most of them entirely out of anyone's control, and plenty of them from *both* sides of the family whether anyone's ready to admit it or not.

**It explains the déjà vu.** If your child's assessment report reads suspiciously like your partner's autobiography, that's not a coincidence and it's not your imagination. That's heritability showing its working.

**It changes how you read your own extended family.** Suddenly Uncle Whoever's "particular ways" and Grandma's noise-cancelling headphones before noise-cancelling headphones were a product category make a lot more sense.

### The bit that actually matters

None of this is about building a case file. It's about understanding that neurodivergence in a family is rarely an isolated event — it's usually a thread that's been running through the fabric for longer than anyone noticed, waiting for the right generation, the right assessment, the right language to name it.

Knowing that doesn't change the parenting in front of you today. But it does take some of the "why us" out of the room, and replace it with something closer to "of course, now that I look properly."
