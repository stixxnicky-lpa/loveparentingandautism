# Family-Friendly Eclipse Guide: Tips, Sensory Advice & Resources for 12 August 2026

On Wednesday 12 August 2026, a total solar eclipse will sweep across parts of Greenland, Iceland, northern Spain and a tiny corner of Portugal. Much of Europe, the UK & Ireland, and parts of North America will see a deep partial eclipse.

For many families this will be a once-in-a-generation moment. But eclipses can also be overwhelming — bright light, crowds, sudden temperature drops, strange animal behaviour, and the need to wear special glasses. Here’s how to make the day calm, safe and memorable for everyone, including children and people with sensory needs.

---

## 1. Safety First: Protect Those Eyes

Never look at the sun without proper protection, even during the partial phases.

- Only use eclipse glasses or handheld viewers that meet the **ISO 12312-2** safety standard.
- Check the glasses before use: no scratches, holes or damage.
- Children often need adult supervision to keep the glasses on. Consider glasses with elastic straps or child-sized frames if available.
- During **totality** (only in the path of totality) you can remove the glasses for the brief period when the sun is completely covered. Put them back on the moment the bright “diamond ring” appears.
- Never look through binoculars, cameras or telescopes with eclipse glasses on — the concentrated light can burn through the filters.

---

## 2. Family Practical Tips

### Plan the location early
Check whether you are in the path of totality or will only see a partial eclipse. Arrive early to claim a good viewing spot and avoid last-minute stress. Have a backup indoor or shaded plan in case of clouds or if a child becomes overwhelmed.

### Timing & comfort
The eclipse lasts a couple of hours from first contact to last contact. Pack water, snacks, sun cream, hats and layers (temperature can drop noticeably near totality). Bring a picnic blanket or folding chairs so younger children can sit or lie down.

Use the partial phases as a countdown game (“Look how much more of the sun is covered!”) to keep interest high.

### Keep it short for little ones
Very young children may only manage 20–40 minutes of focused viewing. That’s fine. The memory of the sky changing is often more powerful than watching the entire event.

---

## 3. Sensory-Friendly Advice

Eclipses can be intense for autistic children, those with ADHD, sensory processing differences, or anxiety.

### Before the day
- Show photos or short videos of previous eclipses so the visual change isn’t a complete surprise.
- Practice putting on and taking off the eclipse glasses at home.
- Talk through what will happen: the light will get dimmer, it might feel cooler, birds may go quiet, and the sky will look strange.

### On the day – reduce overwhelm
- Choose a quieter viewing spot away from large crowds and loud commentary if possible.
- Bring noise-reducing headphones or ear defenders.
- Offer a familiar comfort item (fidget toy, soft scarf, weighted lap pad).
- Allow movement — some children need to walk around or sit under a blanket between looks at the sun.
- Use a simple visual schedule or timer so everyone knows when the “exciting bit” is coming and when it’s nearly over.

### During the partial phases
Looking through eclipse glasses can feel dark and disorienting. Let children take breaks and look at the ground or their hands instead. Some people find the sudden drop in light or the “eerie” quality of the light unsettling. Reassure them it’s temporary and completely normal.

### After the eclipse
Have a calm wind-down activity ready (snack, quiet play, favourite book). Talk about what everyone noticed — the colours, the temperature, how the animals behaved. Processing the experience helps it settle.

## 4. Useful Resources

### Official eclipse information
- NASA: science.nasa.gov (search “August 12 2026 eclipse”)
- TimeandDate.com – interactive maps and exact times for your location
- National Solar Observatory eclipse map

### Safe viewing gear
Look for ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses from reputable sellers. Many astronomy societies and science museums will stock them closer to the date.

### Sensory & neurodiversity support
- Autistic-led or sensory-friendly viewing events (check local astronomy clubs and autism organisations nearer the time).
- Apps such as “Eclipse Guide” or simple countdown timers can help with structure.
- Social stories about eclipses are available from various autism resource sites — search “solar eclipse social story”.

### Activities for kids
- Make a pinhole projector with a cardboard box or two pieces of card (safe alternative to direct viewing).
- Draw the changing shape of the sun every few minutes.
- Note the temperature drop and any animal behaviour changes.

**Final Thought**

The 12 August 2026 eclipse is a rare chance to share wonder as a family. With a bit of preparation, the right glasses, and attention to sensory needs, it can be magical rather than stressful.

Wherever you are watching from — whether you see totality or a deep partial — the most important thing is that everyone feels safe and included.

Clear skies, and enjoy the show 🌑✨
